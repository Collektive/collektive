# The Challenge of Building Resilient Systems

In most distributed systems and **Internet of Things** (IoT) applications,
the **dynamic nature** of the environment and the **unpredictable behavior** of individual devices pose significant challenges.
Often these systems are composed of **heterogeneous** and **resource-constrained** devices,
which may be **mobile** or **static**, and are subject to **frequent failures** and **topology changes**.
Also,
it is common to assume that devices are connected to the Internet to perform their tasks,
but this is not always the case.
For example, in **smart cities** or **smart agriculture** applications,
the devices may be deployed in remote areas with limited connectivity,
or in **disaster recovery** scenarios where the network infrastructure is damaged.

The heterogeneity of the devices can lead to different communication protocols and implementation of the same functionalities,
which can make it difficult to achieve **interoperability** and **scalability**.
Ideally,
we would like to write a single program, describing the behavior of the whole system, that runs on all devices;
this is precisely the goal of **Aggregate Programming**.

## Aggregate Programming as a Relevant Approach

In this context, **Aggregate Programming**[^1] (AP) emerges as a **relevant macroprogramming approach**. It is based on the **functional composition** of **reusable collective behavior blocks**, with the goal of efficiently achieving **complex** and **resilient behaviors** in **dynamic networks**.

### Key Properties of Aggregate Programming

- **Self-organization**: the system as a whole is capable of achieving globally coordinated behaviors through decentralized interactions among individual devices.
    - **Self-coordination**: devices autonomously exchange information and adapt their behavior to maintain coherence, even in the absence of centralized control;
    - **Self-stabilization**: whenever inputs and network stop changing, the system converges to the correct result, whatever the state it starts from: the effects of transient disruptions eventually vanish.
- **Resilience**: the system is inherently robust to node failures, dynamic topology changes, and unpredictable environmental conditions (e.g., wireless interference, sudden weather changes affecting sensor networks, or physical obstacles disrupting communication), ensuring continued functionality under adverse circumstances.
- **Scalability**: each device only interacts with its neighbors, so the communication and computation required on each device do not depend on the size of the whole system, and the same program works with tens or thousands of devices.

Aggregate programming finds its conceptual roots in the **field calculus**[^3] (FC), a minimal functional programming language designed for the specification and composition of collective behaviors. FC provides a formal framework for expressing distributed algorithms in terms of *computational fields*, enabling concise and declarative descriptions of large-scale system behaviors.
Its most recent evolution, the **eXchange Calculus**[^6] (XC), unifies state management, neighbor interaction, and domain partitioning into a single primitive, `exchange`: XC is the calculus implemented by Collektive (see [Aggregate Operators](dsl-descriptions.mdx)).

Building on this foundation, aggregate programming defines **building blocks**[^7], which serve as foundational mechanisms for collective behavior. 

For example, **broadcasting** and **gradient formation** are widely applied in sensor networks to propagate information efficiently. 

**Event detection** and **consensus** play a crucial role in distributed monitoring systems, enabling nodes to agree on the occurrence of specific conditions despite local disruptions. 

Similarly, **clustering** and **leader election** are essential in ad-hoc mobile networks to establish hierarchical structures and coordinate actions. 

These building blocks lay the groundwork for more advanced and resilient applications across dynamic and large-scale environments.

### Practical Applications of Aggregate Programming

Aggregate Programming has demonstrated its versatility in solving complex coordination problems in distributed systems. Practical use cases include:

- **Robot Swarms**[^4]: AP enables coordinated behavior in fleets of autonomous robots, such as formation control, coverage, and search-and-rescue operations, where dynamic and resilient coordination is required.
- **Crowd Management**[^5]: AP supports applications for monitoring and controlling crowds, including density estimation, anomaly detection, and safe dispersal strategies in large-scale events or emergency scenarios.
- **Environmental Monitoring**: sensor networks employing AP can collectively estimate parameters like pollution levels, temperature gradients, or wildfire spread by aggregating local measurements efficiently.
- **Morphogenetic engineering**[^2]: AP can drive the growth of self-organizing spatial structures, as in the field-based vascular morphogenesis controller implemented with Collektive (see the [Showcase](/docs/category/showcase/) for more applications built with Collektive).

These examples highlight how Aggregate Programming simplifies the design and implementation of robust, scalable, and adaptive behaviors in dynamic and decentralized systems.
